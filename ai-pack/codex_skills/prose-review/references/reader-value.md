# Reader-value review

## The central test

Ask: **Would someone who deliberately opened this page want to read it?** Do not answer from grammatical fluency. Answer from what the article helps the reader understand.

## Subject control

The grammatical and conceptual subject should usually be the article subject. Flag paragraphs that begin with the host institution, a curator, a community, or broad cultural scenery and reach the subject only later. This is especially damaging in the lead.

Relationships deserve space in proportion to their sourced importance. A local association can explain inclusion without defining the subject. Remove or relocate it when it crowds out identity, history, work, mechanism, reception, or other subject-specific facts.

## Information-bearing prose

Every paragraph should have a defensible job. Prefer concrete actors, actions, dates, artifacts, mechanisms, and sourced interpretations. Flag:

- generic claims of importance, influence, evolution, or legacy;
- abstract nouns that replace an actual event or mechanism;
- transitions that repeat the preceding sentence;
- “context” that never returns to the subject;
- ornamental quotations;
- conclusions that merely restate the lead;
- paragraph templates that could accept another subject name unchanged.

The problem is not that a phrase is associated with machine writing. The problem is that the sentence spends reader attention without delivering a sourced distinction.

## Shape and pacing

Thin articles should be concise, not inflated. Long articles should earn sections through evidence. Check chronology where it aids comprehension; otherwise group by the subject's real dimensions. Avoid one-sentence sections, duplicate leads, empty appendices, and generic “Impact/Legacy/Future” scaffolding.

Use quotations as evidence of a distinctive claim or voice, not as decoration. Explain why a quotation matters without overstating it.

## Leads

A strong lead:

- identifies the subject immediately;
- distinguishes it from nearby subjects;
- states the most important supported facts;
- reflects the article body and its due weight;
- avoids provenance theater, significance inflation, and local-membership justification.

If the body cannot support a good lead, the problem is usually research or article scope, not lead wording.

## Repair advice

Recommend the smallest repair that restores reader value: delete filler, move a disproportionate relationship, split a compound claim, add a missing explanatory fact, re-sequence around chronology, or return to research. Do not prescribe a total rewrite when local edits suffice.
